In today’s digital age, screens have become a staple in childhood—from tablets used for learning to the latest video games and streaming shows. Here in Gretna, NE, we’re seeing kids spending more hours than ever in front of devices. While most parents are aware of the effects on sleep and focus, one often overlooked concern is how screen habits can contribute to **mouth breathing** — a habit that can impact your child’s dental growth and overall oral health.

At **Platte River Orthodontics**, Dr. Chad Carter frequently observes young patients who breathe through their mouths, and we want to share why this matters and what you can do to help your child maintain a healthy, confident smile.

## How Screen Time Influences Breathing and Posture

When kids hunch over devices, their heads tend to lean forward and their mouths may naturally fall open. This “forward head posture” changes the shape of the airway and makes nasal breathing more difficult, encouraging mouth breathing instead. Over time, this can become a persistent habit—even during nighttime rest.

Of course, mouth breathing isn’t caused by screen time alone. Allergies, enlarged tonsils or adenoids, and nasal blockages also play a role. But the posture kids develop while glued to screens can worsen this issue, affecting how they breathe and how their mouths develop.

## The Effects of Mouth Breathing on Your Child’s Dental Development

A closed mouth with the tongue resting lightly on the roof of the mouth is crucial for proper oral growth. This position helps widen the upper jaw, creating enough space for all permanent teeth to come in straight. When your child breathes through their mouth, the tongue drops away from the palate, reducing this natural expansion. The result can be a **narrow, high-arched palate** that often leads to crowded teeth and the need for orthodontic treatment down the line.

Mouth breathing also dries out the mouth by lowering saliva flow. Since saliva protects teeth by washing away food particles and neutralizing harmful acids, a dry mouth becomes a breeding ground for cavities and gum disease—even if your child brushes regularly.

Beyond teeth, mouth breathing can influence jaw alignment and facial structure, potentially contributing to long-term issues with the jaw joint (TMJ) and bite problems.

## What You Can Do as a Parent

Start by watching your child’s posture and breathing habits, especially when they’re on screens or sleeping. Signs of chronic mouth breathing include dry or chapped lips, frequent snoring, open mouth at rest, and daytime tiredness despite enough sleep.

Encourage frequent breaks from devices, promote good posture, and work with your pediatrician to address allergies or nasal congestion.
